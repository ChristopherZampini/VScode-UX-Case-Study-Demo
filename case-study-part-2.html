Case study · Part 2 · Product concept

A workspace built around the SSP.

A System Security Plan is more than a checklist. This concept organizes the system story, boundary, implementation, people, policies, evidence, and review into one traceable workspace. Each small product window below is a self-contained visual mockup—not a link to the Part 1 demo.

How to use this tour

Follow the numbered outline from workspace overview through supporting records. The proposed structure is intentionally comprehensive; the organization should tailor it to its system, contract, assessment scope, and applicable CMMC level.

Section 01 · Feature

Workspace overview

A landing page gives the team one quick read on the SSP’s health: what has an owner, what needs a decision, and what is coming up for review.

What belongs here

  • System name, selected CMMC level, and current document version
  • Completion counts with explicit “illustrative” or “not assessed” labels
  • Open decisions, overdue reviews, and recently changed records

Section 03 · Define the system

System identification, purpose, and scope

Describe exactly what the plan covers—and what it does not. This is the foundation for deciding which requirements and evidence are relevant.

What belongs here

  • System name, purpose, business function, owner, and operating location
  • Information types handled and the applicable information protection context
  • Included and excluded users, components, facilities, processes, and services
  • Boundary narrative and scope decisions with rationale

Section 04 · Show how it works

Operating environment, architecture, and connections

Describe the technical and operational environment, including how the system boundary relates to other systems and services.

What belongs here

  • Architecture and boundary diagrams with legends and current dates
  • Hardware, software, services, networks, facilities, and deployment locations
  • External connections, data flows, service providers, and shared environments
  • Trust boundaries, remote access, and relevant security assumptions

Section 05 · Assign accountability

Personnel, roles, and responsibilities

Connect system operations to named accountable roles. Include internal teams and external parties without exposing unnecessary personal information.

What belongs here

  • System owner, security lead, administrators, users, and approval authority
  • Responsibility matrix for implementation, evidence, review, and incident escalation
  • Service-provider roles, shared responsibilities, and points of contact
  • Training/awareness expectations and personnel onboarding/offboarding references

Section 06 · Explain implementation

Security requirement implementation

This is the heart of the SSP: explain how each applicable requirement is implemented in the defined system, who is responsible, and where supporting material is maintained. A status by itself is not an implementation statement.

For each requirement, capture

  • Requirement ID and applicable baseline; applicability or tailoring rationale
  • System-specific implementation narrative, technologies, process, and responsible role
  • Inheritance/shared responsibility, dependencies, and referenced policy/evidence
  • Gaps, planned actions, or approved rationale where implementation is not applicable

Section 07 · Document the operating model

Policies, procedures, and operating practices

Policies set expectations; procedures and operating records show how people carry them out. Link them to requirements, owners, approval status, and review dates.

Common supporting material

  • Access control, identification, media, physical security, and configuration practices
  • Incident handling, maintenance, audit, training, and system integrity procedures
  • Document owner, approval/version, effective date, and scheduled review
  • Clear distinction between draft templates and approved organizational policy

Section 08 · Make evidence traceable

Evidence and artifact register

An SSP can reference supporting artifacts without embedding sensitive evidence in an unsafe location. Record enough metadata to find, validate, and review each approved artifact.

What belongs here

  • Artifact name/type, linked requirement, repository reference, and owner
  • Collection period, date, version, review status, and retention/handling notes
  • Evidence sufficiency and relevance reviewed by an accountable person
  • Approved location and access restrictions—never store CUI in this demo

Section 09 · Manage open work

Gaps, risks, and remediation tracking

Keep deficiencies and decisions visible, assign an owner, and track planned actions to closure. Preserve the distinction between preparation status and formal assessment results.

What belongs here

  • Gap/decision description, affected requirement, risk, owner, and discovery date
  • Planned action, due date, dependencies, milestones, and closure evidence
  • Escalations, accepted risks, and approvals documented through governance
  • Separate POA&M handling where required; confirm eligibility and rules before use

Section 10 · Keep it current

Assessment, review, approvals, and change history

Show how the SSP is maintained as the system changes and how implementation statements and evidence are reviewed.

What belongs here

  • Review triggers and cadence; change-impact analysis and change log
  • Approval roles, review evidence, and unresolved questions
  • Assessment/validation records kept distinct from internal workflow status
  • Periodic confirmation of boundary, interconnections, owners, and requirement narratives

Section 11 · Make the plan usable

Appendices, definitions, and references

Keep supporting material organized and navigable. Reference authoritative source documents and version them so reviewers know what the plan relied on.

Useful appendices

  • System inventory, data-flow and boundary diagrams, and glossary
  • Responsibility/shared-service matrix and cross-reference tables
  • Abbreviations, assumptions, scope decisions, and change history
  • Applicable contract clauses, assessment guides, and organizational standards

Why this outline: The SSP should identify the system boundary, operating environment, how applicable security requirements are implemented, and the system’s relationships or connections to other systems. The sections above make those core elements easier to author and maintain. Exact contents, baseline, handling, and assessment expectations depend on the organization’s scope and governing requirements; confirm them with the appropriate stakeholders and current authoritative guidance.
